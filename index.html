Geist

Geist is a research system for people who cover public companies. It keeps the facts, the evidence, and your view of a company together as new information arrives.

Try Geist (Work in Progress)

Research

Ask what a company reported and Geist returns the figure, the fiscal period, and the exact place it came from in the SEC filing.

The evidence is part of the answer. You can open the filing and check it yourself. If Geist cannot establish the fact from the available evidence, it refuses instead of filling in the gap.

Keep the research

A useful answer is usually the beginning of the work, not the end.

You can keep the evidence in a living memo for the company: what you believe, what supports or challenges that view, what could change your mind, and what you want to watch next.

Geist keeps the research organized. The investment view stays yours.

When the company reports again

The next filing should not mean starting over.

Geist brings the new filing back to the research you already have and helps you see what supports the existing view, what contradicts it, and what remains unresolved.

Over time, each filing becomes another piece of evidence in the same body of research.

Why the evidence matters

I asked Perplexity and Geist the same question about Microsoft's reported cloud revenue. They returned different answers.

I assumed Geist was wrong. I checked the filing manually, and Geist had resolved the right figure.

The same question asked to Perplexity

The interesting part was not that one system got a number wrong. It was why.

A number can appear in the right filing and still answer the wrong question. It may belong to a different metric, period, segment, or column in the table. A citation tells you where a number came from. It does not necessarily tell you what that number means.

Geist is built around that distinction.

Models help interpret the question and find relevant information. The filing remains the source of truth. Geist establishes the financial fact against the underlying SEC evidence before returning it.

Ryse, Inc.

At Ryse, I worked on portfolio risk analysis for large fixed-income portfolios.

The starting point was an Excel-based model used to understand how different bonds and risk factors moved together, especially during periods of market stress or major market events. It could answer useful questions, but the process was manual and difficult to extend: adding more assets, testing different assumptions, or rerunning the analysis across new periods required a lot of hands-on work.

I rebuilt the analysis from scratch on Python and turned it into a repeatable pipeline. Instead of working through a small set of positions one at a time, we could process much larger portfolios, test multiple ways of modeling how assets move together, and run simulations to see how losses could behave under different market conditions.

That mattered because portfolio risk is often driven less by what each asset does on its own and more by what happens when many assets move together at the same time. The Python system made it easier to test those relationships consistently, compare results across market regimes, and reuse the same analysis as the portfolio changed.

I later extended the work across the S&P 500 to study how dependence between assets changed during periods such as the tech bubble burst, 2008 financial crisis, COVID, the 2025 tariffs period and everything in between. The research became part of Ryse's risk platform and was later presented at Bloomberg's Quant Seminar Series.
